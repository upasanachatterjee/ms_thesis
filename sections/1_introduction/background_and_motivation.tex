\section{Background and Motivation}

Consider two news outlets reporting on the same event. Table~\ref{fig:khalil} shows how the Associated Press and Fox News each covered the arrest of Columbia University activist Mahmoud Khalil. Despite writing about the same event, their framing diverges in systematic ways. The AP describes ``demonstrators'' who ``filled the lobby'' to ``denounce'' an ``immigration arrest'' of a ``pro-Palestinian activist''; Fox describes ``protesters'' who ``occupied'' the same space, labels Khalil an ``anti-Israel activist,'' and characterises him as ``detained.'' Even the headline verbs encode stance: ``flood'' frames the crowd's action as a surge of feeling; ``occupy'' frames it as a seizure of space. The underlying event is shared; the framing is not. A reader who encounters only one of these accounts receives a systematically different picture of what occurred, and most readers do encounter only one, served by an algorithm optimised for engagement rather than perspectival breadth.

This kind of systematic divergence is not an isolated case. News content has grown increasingly polarised along partisan lines, and algorithmically curated feeds reinforce exposure to ideologically congruent material in ways that amplify rather than moderate that polarisation \citep{flaxman2016filterbubblesechochambers, kitchens2020echochambersfilterbubbles, Garimella_Smith_Weiss_West_2021, cinelli2021echochambereffectsocialmedia}. Readers are generally worse at recognising their own ideological commitments than at recognising those of others \citep{wang2020bias}, and are therefore poorly positioned to self-correct for the framing of the content they consume.

Artificial intelligence is now interposed between journalists and their audiences at every stage of this cycle. Generative systems contribute to reporting, summarisation, and headline generation \citep{al2024artificial, sonnie2025digital}, while recommendation engines, automated summarisers, and conversational interfaces mediate what readers encounter \citep{Rydenfelt19022026, Kiskola_2025}. These capabilities can intensify the problem rather than resolve it. Personalisation optimises for engagement, which tends to reinforce ideological alignment; summarisation pipelines that collapse perspectival variation can erase precisely the framing differences visible in Table~\ref{fig:khalil} and present a homogenised view. A reader querying an AI assistant for a news summary may receive a flattened account that papers over the divergence between the coverage of different outlets, or may receive a summary that reflects the ideological tendencies of the underlying model.

Yet the same AI capabilities that can amplify polarisation can, if deployed carefully, help detect and surface it. Reliable machine modelling of political ideology\footnote{The literature is inconsistent: \citet{ronnback2025biasoutlets}, \citet{baly2020we}, and crowd-sourced resources such as AllSides and Media Bias/Fact Check refer to political ``bias,'' while \citet{liu2022politics} and others use ``ideology.'' When discussing specific works or resources this thesis adopts their original terminology; when describing our own contributions we prefer ``ideology.''} provides an infrastructural layer for media literacy tools that flag ideologically loaded framing, recommendation systems that diversify exposure across the political spectrum, and editorial workflows that audit coverage patterns. Building this infrastructure, however, requires more than a classifier that produces accurate aggregate labels. The target is a classifier whose judgments rest on the same textual evidence a careful human reader would cite: the lexical and framing choices that constitute the metalinguistic cues to ideology illustrated in Table~\ref{fig:khalil}. And verifying that a classifier actually routes through those cues, rather than superficial proxies such as publication names or salient named entities, is not a question that standard accuracy metrics can answer. It requires a causal framework that traces the pathway from text to ideology and compares it across human and machine annotators alike. Together, improved classification and causal auditing constitute the two tightly coupled contributions of this thesis.
