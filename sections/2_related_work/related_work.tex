
\chapter{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

The two contributions of this thesis draw on four partially overlapping literatures, and this chapter reviews them in the order the argument requires. Section~\ref{sec:rw-computational-bias-detection} surveys computational approaches to political bias and ideology detection, including the metalinguistic feature work that motivates our continued pre-training design. Section~\ref{sec:rw-human-annotation} reviews what is known about human annotation for this task and the baselines that inform our lay-annotator study. Section~\ref{sec:rw-llm-surrogates} turns to the increasingly common practice of using large language models as surrogate annotators or direct classifiers, and catalogues the shortcut-learning and contamination risks that make F1-based evaluation insufficient for certifying such systems. Section~\ref{sec:rw-causal-inference-nlp} then reviews causal-inference methods in NLP as the toolkit that makes such certification possible, and closes with Double Machine Learning as the specific estimator used in Chapter~\ref{sec:causal-inference}.

Before proceeding, two preliminary framings are worth establishing. Early work in automated ideology classification was predominantly binary or focused on identifying extreme partisan viewpoints (hyper-partisan news) \citep{hube2018detecting, semeval2019hyperpartisan, krieger2022daroberta, lin2024inditag}. This framing missed center-leaning content and moderate positions, which constitute a substantial fraction of real political discourse and are precisely where ideological signal is hardest to identify. The literature is further distinguished by granularity: sentence-level work \citep{spinde2021mbic, spinde2021babe, menzner2025biasscanner} identifies specific biased phrases but often fails to capture cumulative framing effects across entire articles, whereas article-level classification considers the holistic political orientation of a text and is the granularity we adopt.
